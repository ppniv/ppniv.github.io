About the workshop

Recently, machine intelligence has achieved significant progress, driven by advances in sensing, perception, learning, planning, navigation, and autonomous decision-making. These developments enable autonomous vehicles and robots to understand complex environments, reason under uncertainty, and execute coordinated actions across diverse scenarios. Beyond improving technical performance, a central challenge is achieving effective harmonization among humans, vehicles, robots, and autonomous systems, ensuring that autonomy operates in ways that are transparent, predictable, and aligned with human expectations. As these technologies are increasingly deployed in open-world, on public roadways, and in mission-critical environments, building and maintaining human trust in autonomous systems becomes essential for safe and widespread adoption. In dynamic and uncertain settings, autonomous systems must continuously adapt to changing conditions through robust perception, adaptive planning, and reliable navigation while interacting naturally with human users. Integrating adaptive autonomy, robust control, and human-centered design principles offers promising pathways toward trustworthy and harmonious human–vehicle–robot–autonomy collaboration in real-world applications.

Keynote Talk 2: Coordination and Harmonization of Vehicles under Mixed Human–Machine Autonomy

Abstract: This talk presents some recent advances in the coordination and harmonization of ground vehicles operating under mixed human–machine autonomy, where human-driven, partially automated, and fully autonomous vehicles coexist within shared traffic environments. The talk will discuss control, optimization, coordination, and learning frameworks that enable cooperative interactions among heterogeneous agents while accounting for human behavior, uncertainty, and risks. Emphasis will be placed on achieving safe, efficient, and human-compatible mobility through distributed coordination, personalized automation, and traffic-level harmonization strategies. Applications to connected and automated vehicles, intelligent transportation systems, and emerging mobility ecosystems will also be highlighted.

Keynote Talk 4: Autonomous Teams: Where Learning Meets Control

Abstract: At the frontier of autonomy lies a fundamental question: how can we design teams of agents that reason, act, learn, and memorize cooperatively in dynamic, uncertain environments? In this talk, I will present a theoretical framework grounded in team theory, a mathematical formalism for decentralized stochastic control problems in which multiple agents with asymmetric information cooperate toward a shared objective. I will discuss recent structural results for sequential dynamic team problems with nonclassical information structures, showing how to construct information states that remain invariant to control strategies, thereby enabling dynamic programming decompositions in decentralized settings. These results can aim toward a unifying scientific foundation—what I refer to as the Science of Autonomous Team Intelligence—where teams of agents, whether robotic, vehicular, or human–machine, can reason, act, learn, and memorize collectively, achieving coherent and safe behavior in complex, uncertain environments.

Keynote Talk 8: Key roles of fast machine proprioceptive feedback and fast instability in automotive motion control

Abstract: Humans’ subconscious capabilities are essential for safe motion. Our very fast proprioceptive (muscle) feedback synchronizes limbs for walking and helps recovery in case of slipping or stumbling. Recently developed Control Barrier Function (CBF) based algorithms include a proprioceptive-feedback-like mechanism that serves a similar function for autonomous agents. Instead of human limbs or muscle groups, the entities are automated vehicles executing highway merges and interchanges. Addition of predictor-corrector loops to a CBF safety filter produced several unexpected advantages including (a) ability to reconcile disagreements between independent agents, (b) creation of exponential instability for agility and gridlock avoidance, and (c) resilience to unexpected events (e.g., mechanical failures). The CBF predictor-corrector loops resemble human proprioceptive feedback and are referred to as machine proprioceptive feedback (MPF). The inter-agent instability delivers efficiency, similar to human unstable gait producing efficient walk. It turns out that a higher MPF speed and a higher rate of inter-agent instability, controlled with separate tuning knobs, both improve resilience. While the results are illustrated with highway driving, one could conjecture that the algorithm properties transfer to other multi-agent systems including robots and UAVs.
